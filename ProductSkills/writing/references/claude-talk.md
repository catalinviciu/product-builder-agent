# Claude talk

The cut list. Models pick the likely next word, so they reach for the constructions that appear most often in business prose. The output gestures at significance without giving the reader anything to act on.

The tells are sentence shapes, not vocabulary. Swapping words does not fix them.

## Shapes: delete on sight

**The false dichotomy for emphasis.** "It's not just X, it's Y." Also "that's not X, it's Y", "X is one thing, Y is another", "not because X, but because Y". Pick one and say it.

**Balanced contrast pairs written for rhythm.** "Cheap to measure, hard to trust." "Useful here, useless there." Keep the clause that is true.

**Singling something out for drama.** "The one call it does not make alone is...", "the only piece that matters here is...". Say it plainly: "Which opportunities get committed is decided outside the team."

**Wind-up before the subject.** "While it may seem counterintuitive at first, the data shows..." Also "Here's the thing", "The reality is", "What's interesting is". Subject first.

**Bare noun-phrase fragment plus a claim about it.** "Two problems, both fixable." "Two different bars, and the distinction matters throughout." State problem one.

**Telling the reader it matters.** "This is important because", "it's worth noting", "keep this in mind", "the distinction matters throughout", "this is the key point". Delete. State the thing.

**Announcing or recapping.** "Let's look at", "now for the results:", "let's dive in", "where this leaves us", "in summary", "to recap". Also restating the reader's point before responding to it. Delete.

**Punchy closing fragment for effect.** "Every time." "Simple as that." "And that changes everything." Delete. The paragraph already ended.

**Sentences that only sound clever about something already explained.** Delete.

**Sentences that only set up the next sentence.** Delete.

**Rule of three as filler.** Three items where two are real and the third completes the rhythm. Three bullets making the same point in different words.

**Crediting the reader mid-answer.** "You're absolutely right", "good catch", "fair point", "as you said", "to your point". Make the change and move on.

**Labelling your own reasoning.** "The big one", "the real problem", "worth noting", "two mitigations that actually work". Also naming a concept, defining it, then justifying it. State it.

## Words that pad

**Hedging verbs used as filler.** Ensures, highlights, supports, reflects, leverages, underscores, showcases, delves into, fosters, facilitates. "Ensuring" is the single strongest tell.

**Intensifiers doing no work.** Quietly, silently, subtly, invisibly, seamlessly, truly, deeply, incredibly, remarkably.

**Vague nouns.** Robust, holistic, alignment, synergy, ecosystem, landscape, framework when it is just a list, solution when you mean the thing.

**Empty transitions.** Furthermore, moreover, additionally, that said, importantly, notably, ultimately, at the end of the day.

**Hedges.** "It seems", "arguably", "somewhat", "relatively", "in many cases", unless the uncertainty is real and load-bearing.

## Habits

- **Avoiding contractions.** Use them: didn't, isn't, can't, won't. Spell the words out only when the emphasis is real, as in "we did not agree to that". A whole reply of "do not / is not / cannot" is a tell on its own.
- **Em dashes.** Plain dash instead. Also no curly quotes or other non-ASCII, which break CSV in Excel on Windows.
- **Giving numbers agency.** A figure that collapses, drifts, masks, erodes, or does anything quietly. Say what a person did and what the number then showed. "If we drop the hard ideas, the average looks better" beats "abandoned work vanishes from the number".
- **Clever or label-style headings.** "The big one", "Conceded", "Where this leaves us". Use plain descriptive headings: "What to change", "Business outcome".
- **Tagging a heading.** ", plain", ", the short version", "(honestly)".
- **Bolded mini-headings inside bullets.**
- **Colon-and-dash rhythm to sound punchy.** Write normal sentences.
- **Emoji as section markers.**

## The caution

Run this list mechanically and you strip voice out of writing that was fine. Em dash frequency alone proves nothing: Mark Twain used them at roughly the same rate as GPT-4.1, and some models use none. The list finds sentences with no content. It is not a punctuation policy.

## The test

For every sentence: what can the reader now do, decide or believe that they could not before? Nothing means delete.
