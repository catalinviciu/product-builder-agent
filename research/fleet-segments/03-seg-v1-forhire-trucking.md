# For-Hire Trucking / Freight Transportation (US) — Segment Measurement Research

**Scope:** ~3.9M power units. Sub-models covered: truckload (dry van, reefer, flatbed), LTL, dedicated contract carriage (DCC), owner-operators/small carriers (1–10 trucks), brokers/3PLs (asset-touching metrics only).

**Method note:** WebFetch was unavailable in this environment. All findings below come from `WebSearch` result snippets — i.e., secondary reporting of primary sources, not direct reads of the underlying studies/filings. Every number is tagged with a confidence label per the instructions:
🟢 primary-sourced (I read the primary document/data directly) — **not achievable this session, so this label is not used below**
🟡 secondary reporting (trade press, vendor blog, or search snippet accurately citing a named primary study/filing)
🔴 unverified industry lore (repeated across vendor content with no identifiable primary study)

Because no primary document was actually read (only snippets), even numbers traceable to ATRI/ATA/FMCSA are capped at 🟡 rather than 🟢 — treat 🟡 here as "a named, credible primary source appears to exist and is cited," not "verified by direct inspection."

## 1. The Operating Model

**How the money actually moves, by sub-model:**

- **Truckload (dry van/reefer/flatbed):** Paid per load or per mile for point-to-point moves, one shipper's freight fills the trailer. Revenue is a function of rate-per-mile × loaded miles, netted against deadhead (unpaid) miles. Contract freight (annual RFP-awarded lanes, fixed or index-linked rate) vs. spot freight (day-to-day, DAT/Truckstop board or broker-tendered, rate floats with capacity). Typical large-fleet mix is majority contract with a spot "relief valve" for network gaps; small carriers/owner-operators lean spot-heavy. Operating ratio (OR = operating expenses ÷ operating revenue) for truckload has been running **at or above 100%** industry-wide through the 2023–2025 freight recession — e.g., Marten Transport's truckload segment OR was **99.8% in 2025** 🟡 (SEC 10-Q/annual report as cited by trade press), and truckload/refrigerated segment operating margins broadly stayed **below 1.0%** in 2025 🟡 (Commercial Carrier Journal, citing Q3 2025 public-carrier earnings, 2025). A "good week" is one where loaded miles are maximized, deadhead is minimized, and the truck picks up its next load within hours of delivery (no dwell); a "bad week" is a truck sitting empty waiting on a load, deadheading to reposition, or detained unpaid at a dock.
- **LTL:** Does not sell truckloads — sells shipments (often <10,000 lbs) consolidated with other shippers' freight through a hub-and-spoke network (local pickup → breakbulk/terminal linehaul → local delivery). Revenue is per-shipment/per-hundredweight (CWT), heavily influenced by freight class/density, not per-mile. Because of network density and pricing power, LTL runs structurally better margins than truckload: LTL carriers averaged an **11.6% operating margin as a sector in 2025** 🟡 (trade press aggregation of public LTL carrier results, 2025), with XPO's North American LTL adjusted OR at **82.7%** 🟡 (same reporting, 2025) — vs. truckload sitting near or above 100% OR. LTL cost per mile also runs materially higher than truckload (**$2.58/mile average in 2025 vs. $2.21/mile for truckload**, 🟡 CCJ/trade press citing carrier data, 2025) because of the terminal handling network, not because the freight itself is expensive to haul.
- **Dedicated contract carriage (DCC):** A shipper contracts for exclusive use of trucks/drivers on defined lanes/schedules, in exchange for revenue and asset-utilization predictability the carrier gives up spot-market upside for. Reported at the segment level by public carriers — Werner's Dedicated segment revenue-per-truck-per-week rose to **$4,789, +5.4% YoY**, vs. Werner's One-Way Truckload segment at **$6,114/truck/week, +27.7% YoY** 🟡 (FreightWaves reporting on Werner quarterly results, 2025/2026) — dedicated trades top-line per-truck revenue for lower variance and lower deadhead (dedicated deadhead runs an estimated **10–20%** vs. 28–45% for open dry van/reefer/flatbed, 🔴 figure appears only in vendor/blog aggregations, no named primary study identified).
- **Owner-operators / small carriers (1–10 trucks):** Either leased to a carrier (paid a percentage of the linehaul, typically **70–85%** before the owner-operator's own truck expenses, 🟡 industry compensation-guide aggregation, 2025/2026) or running under their own authority (keep 100% of revenue but bear 100% of fixed costs, insurance, and back-office burden, and are most exposed to spot-market and deadhead risk). Asset vs. non-asset: these are the purest asset-heavy operators in the segment — one truck down is existential.
- **Brokers/3PLs (asset-touching only):** Non-asset by definition, but they set the market clearing price that determines truckload utilization and deadhead outcomes for the asset carriers they book. Their P&L is gross margin per load (shipper rate minus carrier rate), not cost-per-mile. A brokerage needs roughly **$210–215 in gross margin per load just to break even**, with typical worked examples showing shipper $2,100 / carrier $1,800 = **$300 margin (14.3%)** against **$120–160 operating cost per load** 🔴 (single vendor blog, unnamed methodology — treat as illustrative, not benchmark).
- **Driver pay models across the segment:** cents-per-mile (CPM) — market base **$0.60+/mile for a 2–5-year-experience driver in 2026** 🔴 (industry pay-guide site, no named wage survey); percentage-of-load — company drivers **20–35%** of linehaul revenue, owner-operators leased on **70–85%** 🟡/🔴 mixed sourcing, several pay-guide sites converge on similar ranges but none cite a wage survey; hourly — **$20–35/hr**, more common in LTL city/P&D and drayage. Accessorial pay (detention, layover, tarp, stop pay) supplements base pay and is a direct lever on driver retention.
- **Deadhead economics:** Every empty mile is a mile the truck burns fuel, wears equipment, and pays (or partially pays) the driver, with zero revenue. This is why deadhead % (Section 2) is arguably the single most-watched truckload-specific efficiency metric — it is the direct gap between miles driven and miles paid.

## 3. How They Measure — Cadence, Systems of Record, Data Flow

**Reporting cadence (typical mid-size-to-large truckload/LTL carrier):**
- **Daily:** Dispatch board review (loads covered vs. open, trucks available, on-time exception list), fuel purchases/fuel card exceptions, HOS/ELD violation alerts, safety-camera event review (harsh braking, following distance) — reviewed by dispatch/fleet managers and safety managers.
- **Weekly:** Revenue per truck per week (the TCA Profitability Program's core cadence — explicitly a weekly metric), driver settlements/pay runs, tender acceptance/rejection trends, deadhead % by terminal/region, maintenance shop backlog — reviewed by VP Operations, Director of Maintenance.
- **Monthly:** Full P&L by terminal/business unit, operating ratio, claims ratio, CSA BASIC percentile snapshot, driver turnover rate, insurance loss-run review — reviewed by CFO, VP Safety, ops leadership as a group (monthly ops review / S&OP-style meeting).
- **Quarterly:** Public-carrier earnings calls (for the SEC-reporting fleets — Knight-Swift, Werner, Marten, Schneider, XPO, Old Dominion, etc. — a major indirect source of segment benchmarks since they disclose revenue/truck/week, OR, and deadhead by segment), customer/carrier scorecard business reviews for dedicated and contract accounts, ATRI/ATA benchmark releases land roughly annually-to-semi-annually and get folded into board/ownership reviews.

**Systems of record and how data flows:**
- **TMS (Transportation Management System):** The commercial system of record — McLeod (LoadMaster for truckload carriers, PowerBroker for brokers/3PLs), TMW/Trimble, and others hold load, rate, billing, and settlement data. McLeod's own materials describe reporting/analytics modules (MPact.IQ) and AR breakdowns by lane/customer/service as the mechanism carriers use to see "where revenue comes from" 🟡 (McLeod Software product pages, 2025/2026).
- **ELD/telematics (Motive, Samsara, Omnitracs):** System of record for HOS, location, idle time, harsh-driving events, and (increasingly) fuel/MPG telemetry. Samsara reported **~$1.26B ARR** and Motive **~$501M ARR (Q3 2025)** — both have expanded from pure ELD compliance into dash-cams, maintenance, and spend management (a "land and expand" pattern), meaning telematics platforms are increasingly also claiming ownership of maintenance and safety data that used to live in separate systems 🟡 (company-reported ARR figures via trade/industry press, 2025).
- **Maintenance systems (TMT, Dossier):** System of record for PM schedules, repair orders, parts, and (ideally) cost-per-unit history — though the task brief's known pain point (attributing maintenance cost to a specific tractor across a rotating driver pool) is a real reconciliation gap between this system and dispatch/TMS records.
- **Fuel cards (Comdata, WEX):** System of record for fuel purchase price, gallons, location — feeds both MPG calculation (cross-referenced against ELD odometer/miles) and IFTA fuel-tax reporting.
- **Safety/camera (Lytx, SambaSafety):** System of record for driver risk scoring, video event review, and (SambaSafety specifically) MVR/CSA monitoring — feeds the Safety Manager's daily/weekly review and often feeds insurance underwriting directly.
- **Reconciliation reality:** No single system is authoritative for "how many miles did this truck actually run." IFTA filings are supposed to be built from the same trip/load records used for billing and driver pay, but in practice a quarterly IFTA filing often requires a manual ELD export cross-referenced against TMS load records and fuel-card data — three separate systems that "just don't agree" 🟡 (compliance-software vendor content describing the reconciliation workflow, 2026). A **0.5–2% variance** between GPS/ELD miles and odometer miles per quarter is described as normal/acceptable, with **5%+ triggering an audit flag** 🔴 (single compliance-tool vendor site, no named regulatory source for these specific thresholds).
- **BI layer vs. spreadsheets:** Larger carriers increasingly pull TMS + ELD + maintenance + fuel-card data into a BI layer (custom or vendor, e.g., McLeod's MPact.IQ) for cross-system KPI dashboards. Small carriers (1–10 trucks) very commonly run on a single, hand-built spreadsheet rather than any dedicated system — explicitly documented as a common practice ("a single, well-built spreadsheet... can run 90% of a small fleet's back office") 🟡 (FreightWaves feature, small-fleet management practices, cited alongside multiple dedicated micro-fleet tools like Rigbooks, TruckingOffice, HaulMargin built specifically because owner-operators are underserved by enterprise TMS).

## 4. Who Owns Each Number

| Role | Metrics owned / primarily reviewed | Typically bonused on |
|---|---|---|
| CEO / Owner (esp. small carrier) | OR, revenue/truck/week, overall cash position — often the *only* numbers tracked at 1–10 truck scale | N/A (owner is the profit-taker) |
| CFO | Operating ratio, cost per mile (all-in and by category), insurance cost per unit, claims ratio, working capital/DSO | Company-wide OR / net margin targets |
| VP Operations | Revenue per truck per week, deadhead %, tender acceptance rate, on-time pickup/delivery, trailer-to-tractor ratio, driver utilization | Revenue/truck/week, OR, on-time performance |
| VP Safety | CSA BASIC percentiles, DOT recordable/preventable accident rate, HOS violation rate, insurance loss-run trends | CSA score improvement, crash-rate reduction, insurance renewal outcome |
| Director of Maintenance | Maintenance cost per mile, uptime/downtime, PM compliance %, "Vehicle Maintenance: Driver Observed" violations (new 2026 category) | Cost/mile vs. budget, downtime reduction |
| Dispatch / Fleet Manager | Daily load coverage, deadhead % by lane, on-time pickup/delivery, tender rejection decisions, driver HOS availability | Load coverage %, on-time %, sometimes revenue/truck/week at the terminal level |
| Driver Manager | Driver turnover, driver utilization (miles/truck/week), driver pay accuracy/settlement disputes, detention capture at the load level | Driver retention rate, fleet utilization |
| Safety Manager | Daily camera-event review, HOS violation alerts, roadside inspection outcomes, driver coaching completion | Reduction in violations/events, inspection pass rate |

Note: at the 1–10 truck scale these roles collapse into 1–2 people (often the owner + a spouse/office manager), which is exactly why Section 6's "small carriers measure almost nothing beyond cash" finding matters for product design — there is no Director of Maintenance persona to sell a maintenance-cost-per-mile dashboard to.

## 6. Where Measurement Breaks Down Today

1. **ELD vs. TMS vs. IFTA vs. odometer miles never fully reconcile.** These four systems each calculate "miles" slightly differently (ELD GPS pings and jurisdiction-crossing logic vs. TMS-planned route miles used for customer billing vs. IFTA jurisdiction-mile totals vs. physical odometer). A quarterly IFTA filing commonly requires manual export-and-cross-reference across three systems, and even a "normal" 0.5–2% variance between GPS and odometer miles compounds into real dollars at scale — a 5%+ variance is described as an audit trigger 🟡/🔴 (mechanism well-documented across multiple compliance-tool vendors; the specific numeric thresholds are less consistently sourced). **This is a genuine, structural pain point, not lore** — it recurs across every source describing IFTA/mileage reconciliation workflows.

2. **True cost-per-mile by lane/customer/driver, not fleet average.** The ATRI $2.336/mile figure is a fleet-wide average; it says nothing about whether a specific lane, customer, or driver is profitable. TMS platforms (McLeod, TMW) can theoretically break AR down by lane/customer, but tying that revenue-side view to a *fully loaded, driver- and lane-specific* cost side (fuel, maintenance attributed to the specific tractor, driver pay, deadhead incurred getting to that lane) is not something any source described as a solved, standard workflow — it remains largely a BI/analytics-layer aspiration rather than an out-of-the-box TMS report.

3. **Maintenance cost attribution across a rotating driver pool.** Named explicitly in the task brief as a known issue; search did not surface a documented industry-standard solution. The structural problem: maintenance systems (TMT, Dossier) key cost to the *unit* (tractor), which is correct, but diagnosing whether a maintenance spike is unit-caused (bad component) or driver-caused (harsh driving, poor pre-trip inspections) requires joining maintenance records to whichever driver was assigned to that unit on which dates — a join that telematics/safety platforms (Lytx, Samsara) are only beginning to make by cross-referencing driver-ID login events with unit maintenance logs. No source described this as commonly solved today.

4. **Detention capture and billing is broken at scale, and this is well-documented, not lore.** ATRI's own detention study shows shippers are billed *below* the carrier's own operating cost per hour of detention (~$63/hr billed vs. $66.65/hr cost), and **fewer than half of detention invoices actually get paid** — meaning even when carriers correctly measure and invoice detention, the money frequently doesn't come back. This is a collections/contract-enforcement failure layered on top of a measurement failure.

5. **Small carriers (1–10 trucks) largely don't measure anything beyond cash/bank balance.** Multiple sources converge on this: dedicated micro-fleet tools (Rigbooks, TruckingOffice, HaulMargin — explicitly built for 1–5-truck operators) exist *because* mainstream TMS platforms are overbuilt for this segment, and the most commonly cited practice is "a single spreadsheet running 90% of the back office." The metrics small operators do track when they track anything are basic: cost-per-mile, fuel cost/MPG, and net profit per load — not OR, not CSA percentiles, not turnover (they often *are* the only driver). This is a genuine, confirmed finding, not an assumption.

6. **The 2026 FMCSA CSA/SMS overhaul changes what "tracking safety" means.** Collapsing ~950+ violation codes into ~116 groups with two-tier severity (OOS=2, other=1) means each surviving violation now carries proportionally more scoring weight — carriers reported score changes purely from the recalculation, independent of any change in actual safety behavior. The new "Vehicle Maintenance: Driver Observed" category specifically isolates defects a driver should have caught on pre-trip/post-trip inspection from defects only found by mechanics/inspectors — this creates a new, trackable KPI (driver-observed defect rate) that didn't cleanly exist before, and shifts some safety accountability from Maintenance back onto Driver Managers/Safety Managers for pre-trip inspection discipline. The Driver Fitness intervention threshold reportedly tightens from the 80th to the 90th percentile. **Net effect for fleet software:** any product that shows carriers a CSA percentile trendline needs a hard cutover/re-baseline at the 2026 transition point, and should not chart pre- and post-overhaul percentiles on the same continuous axis without a clear annotation, because the underlying scoring methodology itself changed, not just the carrier's behavior.

7. **The uptime/idle%/PM-compliance numbers are lore, confirmed.** Direct search for primary sources behind "95% uptime," "20–25% idle," and "95% PM compliance" returned only fleet-management-software vendor blogs (OxMaint, Upper, FleetRabbit, Heavy Vehicle Inspection) citing each other or citing nothing, with wide internal inconsistency (idle benchmarks ranged from 5% to 50%+ across sources; uptime ranged 92–96.2%; PM compliance "average" vs. "top performer" gaps were asserted without a named survey). **Recommendation for the product team: do not embed these numbers as authoritative benchmarks in-product; if used at all, label them explicitly as "commonly cited industry range, source unverified."**

## 7. Sub-Model Comparison Table

| Dimension | Truckload (dry van/reefer/flatbed) | LTL | Dedicated Contract Carriage | Owner-Operator / Small Carrier (1–10 trucks) | Broker / 3PL (asset-touching) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue unit | Per load / per mile | Per shipment / per CWT (freight class + density driven) | Per truck/week (contracted), often with fixed + variable components | Per load / per mile (often percentage-of-linehaul if leased) | Per load, gross margin (shipper rate − carrier rate) |
| Core P&L metric | Operating ratio, revenue/truck/week | Operating ratio, cost/shipment, terminal productivity | Revenue/truck/week (contract-guaranteed baseline) | Net profit per load, cost per mile | Gross margin per load, margin % |
| 2025 OR / margin benchmark | ~99.8–100%+ OR (near/below breakeven) | ~88.4% OR / 11.6% margin (sector avg) | Not separately benchmarked in search; embedded in parent carrier's segment reporting (e.g., Werner Dedicated) | Rarely calculated formally; owner tracks cash, not OR | N/A (different P&L structure entirely — margin, not OR) |
| Cost/mile 2025 | $2.21/mile | $2.58/mile | Not separately reported | Highly variable; small carriers often unaware of true all-in CPM | N/A (no linehaul cost; cost is SG&A per load) |
| Key efficiency metric | Deadhead % (16.5% non-tank avg, ATRI 2025) | Freight density, terminal dock productivity, P&D route efficiency | Deadhead % (est. 10–20%, lower than open market — 🔴 unverified) | Deadhead %, but often tracked informally/not at all | Tender acceptance rate of carrier network, load-to-truck ratio |
| Driver turnover 2025/26 | 90–95% (large fleets) | 11–15% | Generally lower than open-market TL due to home-time predictability (no confirmed number found) | Not applicable in the fleet-turnover sense — owner-operator often is the driver | N/A |
| Primary system of record | TMS (McLeod LoadMaster, TMW/Trimble) + ELD | TMS with terminal/dock module + linehaul planning system | Same TMS as parent carrier, often with dedicated customer-facing scorecard/BI layer | Spreadsheet, or micro-fleet tools (Rigbooks, TruckingOffice, HaulMargin) | TMS/brokerage platform (McLeod PowerBroker, DAT, Truckstop) |
| Contract vs. spot mix | Mixed, contract-heavy at large fleets, spot-heavy at small carriers | Almost entirely contract/tariff-based, less spot-exposed | 100% contract by definition | Spot-heavy (especially non-leased authority holders) | Both — books both contract lanes and spot loads for shippers |
| Safety/CSA exposure | Full exposure — this is the core segment CSA was built around | Lower crash exposure per mile due to regional/local route profile, but P&D density adds different risk (frequent stops, urban driving) | Similar to TL but often better because of route familiarity/consistency | Highest per-truck exposure — one bad inspection can knock out the entire operation's authority | Indirect — brokers face vicarious liability/negligent-selection risk from carriers they book, not direct CSA scoring |

**Do not blur these:** an LTL fleet manager who obsesses over deadhead % is looking at the wrong number — LTL's structural cost driver is network/terminal density and freight classification, not empty miles in the truckload sense. Conversely, a truckload dispatcher tracking "cost per shipment" is using an LTL-native concept that doesn't map cleanly onto full-load freight.
