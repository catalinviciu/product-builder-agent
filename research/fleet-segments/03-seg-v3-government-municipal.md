## 1. The Operating Model

Government fleets are **cost centers with no P&L and no competitor**. They are judged on stewardship, availability, compliance, and audit defensibility — not profit. Four structural features distinguish this segment from commercial fleets:

**Internal Service Funds (ISF) and chargeback.** Most city/county/state fleet operations run as an ISF: a government accounting structure where the fleet department bills user departments (police, public works, parks) for vehicle use and should net to roughly break-even at year-end, not generate profit. 🟡 (GFOA guidance, via government-fleet.com and GFOA.org snippets, 2024–2025.) Rates are typically two-part — a fixed monthly capital-recovery charge plus a variable per-mile or per-hour operating charge — intended to recover fully burdened costs (parts at cost plus overhead, labor at fully burdened rate including benefits, admin overhead allocated). 🟡 Source: RTA Fleet blog citing GFOA rate-development principles.

**Capital/operating budget split — the core distortion.** Vehicle *purchase* is typically capital-appropriated (bond funds, capital improvement budgets, one-time council/legislature approval), while *maintenance* is an operating-budget line renewed annually. When capital budgets are frozen, cut, or subject to competing priorities (roads, schools, public safety staffing), replacement is deferred — but the maintenance budget, which is easier to protect politically because it funds ongoing service, absorbs the resulting cost of keeping aging vehicles running. This is the mechanism GAO and Mercury Associates-style consultants point to: deferred replacement → rising repair frequency and cost → downtime → departments hoard backup vehicles → utilization looks worse → the audit finds "underutilized" vehicles that were only kept as a hedge against a fleet office that can't reliably replace failing units. 🟡 (synthesis of GAO GAO-17-426, GAO-16-136 findings plus Mercury Associates ORCA framing — GAO reports 🟢, synthesis is analytical, not directly sourced).

**Motor pool vs. assigned-vehicle models.** Agencies mix dedicated take-home/assigned vehicles (common in police, code enforcement, inspectors) with shared motor pools (dispatched via reservation systems). Motor pools are the lever for right-sizing but are politically harder to expand because departments prefer guaranteed access over shared risk — a recurring audit tension (Salt Lake County audit: 7 of 11 motor-pool vehicles unused in 2025 🟡 citizenportal.ai; Tampa 27% underutilized 🔴 unsourced in snippet).

**GSA leasing vs. agency ownership (federal).** Federal civilian agencies choose between GSA Fleet full-service leasing (200,000+ vehicles under GSA Fleet management, all-inclusive lease rates covering acquisition, maintenance, fuel program administration) 🟡 (gsa.gov snippet) and agency-owned fleets managed on GSAFleet.gov's agency-owned FMIS module. Leasing shifts capital risk to GSA and smooths replacement (GSA sets replacement cycles by vehicle class), while agency ownership retains the same capital/operating split problem described above internally. GAO has repeatedly found leased-vehicle utilization gaps (GAO-16-136: 5 agencies, up to 47% of National Park Service vehicles failed utilization criteria, ~$8.7M at risk 🟢) since GSA lease costs are fixed and continue billing regardless of use, making underuse directly visible in the chargeback.

**Procurement is a 18–36 month cooperative-contract cycle**, not a market transaction. Most sub-federal agencies buy vehicles and fleet management technology off Sourcewell or NASPO ValuePoint cooperative contracts (competitively solicited once, usable by any of Sourcewell's 50,000+ member agencies without a separate RFP) 🟡 (sourcewell.org, NASPO ValuePoint snippets). This collapses sales-cycle economics for a software vendor: the buying unit is the cooperative contract award, not the individual city, and incumbency plus contract vehicle placement matter more than point-in-time competitive displacement.

**Political economy.** Fleet budgets are approved by elected bodies (city council, county commission, state legislature) or an appointed city/county manager acting on their behalf, not a CFO optimizing margin. Budget defense happens in public hearings; the fleet director's currency is a defensible number, not profitability. This is why audit findings (GAO, state auditor, city auditor) function as the de facto external performance review this segment actually fears — closer to a compliance regime than a market discipline.

## 3. How They Measure — Cadence, Workflow, Systems of Record

**Cadence:**
- **Monthly**: department chargeback invoices/reports generated from the FMIS; fuel and mileage capture (federal agencies report mileage via GSA Fleet Drive-Thru web portal for billing) 🟡 (gsa.gov snippet).
- **Annual**: budget justification cycle (capital replacement requests submitted to council/legislature/OMB); federal agencies submit annual fleet data to FAST (Federal Automotive Statistical Tool), which rolls up into GSA's Federal Fleet Report, a year-end snapshot of inventory, cost, fuel, and use data across agencies 🟢 (gsa.gov, catalog.data.gov — FFR described as promoting "accountability" and "transparency across Government").
- **Audit cycle**: GAO performs federal fleet reviews periodically (GAO-04-664, GAO-14-443, GAO-16-136, GAO-17-426, GAO-23-105350/105635, GAO-25-106972 — a roughly biennial cadence on utilization/ZEV topics) 🟢; state auditors and city/county internal/external auditors run their own cycles (e.g., San Diego 2025 fleet audit, Seattle SPD fleet management audit) 🟡.
- **Award/benchmarking cycle**: annual submissions to NAFA's 100 Best Fleets and Government Fleet/APWA's Leading Fleets (2026 cohorts confirmed active) 🟡.

**Systems of record (confirmed via search):**
- **FMIS**: AssetWorks FleetFocus (550+ organizations across North America/UK per AssetWorks; note AssetWorks routes government buyers to a separate "Enterprise Asset Management" brand where fleet sits alongside roads/facilities/parks) 🟡; FASTER (AssetWorks) — "FASTER Web" sunset in summer 2025, folded into FleetFocus 🟡; RTA Fleet360 — markets itself as public-sector-first ("government listed first") 🟡; Chevin FleetWave, CentralSquare, Collective Data — named in the task brief as segment incumbents but not independently confirmed with fresh detail in this search pass (no new snippets beyond general product pages).
- **Federal-specific**: GSAFleet.gov (agency-owned FMIS, replaced FMVRS), GSA Fleet Drive-Thru (mileage reporting/billing portal), Vehicle Dispatch & Reservation Module (DRM) for motor-pool scheduling 🟢 (gsa.gov).
- **Federal statistical reporting**: FAST (Federal Automotive Statistical Tool), co-sponsored by GSA and DOE, feeding the annual Federal Fleet Report 🟢 (energy.gov, gsa.gov).
- **Telematics**: Geotab holds a GSA single-source contract to equip 200,000+ (subsequently expanded to cover 400,000+) GSA-leased and agency-owned vehicles — described as the largest telematics contract GSA has awarded; Geotab is FedRAMP-authorized and FIPS 140-2 validated 🟢 (geotab.com press release, gsa.gov). GSA reported ~$1.5M in savings from activated telematics as of a June 2022 release 🟢 (gsa.gov press release). Verizon Connect named in task brief as a competing telematics vendor in this space; not independently confirmed with new detail this pass.
- **Cooperative procurement contracts** (not a "system of record" but functionally the sales/reporting channel): Sourcewell and NASPO ValuePoint carry fleet management software, telematics (Geotab explicitly sells via both), and vehicle/chassis contracts 🟡 (sourcewell.org, geotab.com blog).

**How data is assembled for audits and public reporting:** Federal agencies submit standardized annual data into FAST → GSA aggregates into the Federal Fleet Report and an open dataset library (catalog.data.gov) for public transparency 🟢. Sub-federal agencies typically export FMIS data (utilization, cost, PM compliance) into a council/board budget-justification packet; auditors independently pull FMIS records and physically verify vehicle counts/usage against them (as in the Salt Lake County and San Diego audits referenced above) 🟡.

## 6. Where Measurement Breaks Down Today

1. **Capital/operating budget separation defeats lifecycle-cost logic.** The fleet director can build an airtight ORCA-style replacement business case, but if the capital budget is appropriated separately (and often less predictably) than the operating/maintenance budget, the "right" replacement year is whatever year capital happens to be available — not the data-optimal year. The result (rising maintenance cost, falling availability) then shows up as a *maintenance budget* problem, obscuring its true *capital budget* cause, and making it hard to trace an audit finding back to its actual root. 🟡 synthesis from GAO/Mercury Associates framing.

2. **Right-sizing is provable in aggregate but politically resisted vehicle-by-vehicle.** GAO's own case studies (GAO-16-136, GAO-17-426) show departments keep vehicles without "readily available justification" — the recommendation in both reports was essentially "require documented justification," which is a process fix, not a data fix. The underlying tension: a department that gives up a vehicle it "barely uses" bears 100% of the risk if it's needed unexpectedly, while the fleet office bears 100% of the audit criticism if it isn't returned. Utilization data alone doesn't resolve this incentive misalignment. 🟢 (GAO findings) / 🟡 (interpretive framing).

3. **Chargeback rates rarely reflect true cost**, both because ISF rate-setting is often a stale, infrequently-revisited process (GFOA specifically flags the need for periodic review as costs change) and because averaging rates across a vehicle class hides the fact that specific vehicles (aging, heavily-upfitted, or high-idle) cost far more than the class average — meaning heavy users of below-average-cost vehicles cross-subsidize others. 🟡.

4. **Police/fire vehicles make standard cost-per-mile meaningless.** A patrol vehicle idling 40-60% of on-scene time consumes fuel and engine hours with no mileage accrual; upfit equipment (light bars, radios, prisoner partitions, computers) adds tens of thousands in acquisition cost not reflected in a per-mile figure; duty cycle is bursty and mission-critical rather than steady-state. Fleet teams reportedly need engine-hours-based or VEU-normalized metrics instead of raw CPM for this class — Mercury Associates' VEU framework is the most credible tool found for this in this research pass. 🟡.

5. **EV total-cost-of-ownership comparisons are structurally split across departments.** Charging infrastructure is frequently a facilities or public-works capital project, not a fleet capital line — meaning the fleet office can show a favorable EV operating-cost case (lower per-mile fuel/maintenance) while the capital cost of making that possible (charger installation, electrical service upgrades) sits in a different department's budget and a different budget cycle. GSA's own gap between installed charging ports (~10,500 in 2024) and its stated eventual need (100,000+) illustrates this at federal scale; the same structural split recurs at city/county level between fleet and facilities/public-works. 🟢 (GSA/GAO figures) / 🟡 (city-level generalization).

6. **Federal EV mandate reversal creates a measurement discontinuity.** EO 14057's 2027/2035 ZEV acquisition targets were revoked in January 2025, but GSA had already ordered ~82,000 EVs and built substantial charging infrastructure under the old mandate — leaving agencies measuring progress against a target that no longer formally exists, while still holding the sunk assets. This is a live, current (2025-2026) measurement-framework problem specific to this segment's political vulnerability. 🟡 (press coverage of the EO revocation; GAO's most recent report, GAO-25-106972, appears to still be assessing the now-superseded ZEV transition).

7. **Audit findings recur year after year** because they're driven by structural incentives (see #2 above) rather than data availability — more granular utilization data does not, by itself, change a department's incentive to hoard a vehicle. Search did not surface a case of "recommendation implemented, finding resolved" at scale, only new fleets or new years surfacing the same underutilization pattern.
