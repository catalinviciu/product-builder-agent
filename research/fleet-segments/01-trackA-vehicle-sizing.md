# Track A — US Fleet Management Software: Powered On-Road Vehicle Sizing by Vertical

**Prepared:** September 2026. **Scope (revised):** US business/commercial/public-sector fleets of **powered on-road vehicles only** — Class 1-8 trucks, vans, pickups, cars, and buses. Non-powered/off-road assets (trailers, containers, heavy equipment, forklifts, generators, light towers, ag equipment) are **explicitly out of scope** — a parallel workstream owns that analysis; this document does not rank or size them.

**Research method note:** All numbers come from `WebSearch` result snippets. `WebFetch` was blocked by network egress policy for every domain attempted this session (automotive-fleet.com, fleetowner.com, berginsight.com, sec.gov, fhwa.dot.gov, trucking.org, wikipedia.org, globenewswire.com, contractormag.com, iotbusinessnews.com), so no primary document could be opened and read directly — every figure below is a second-hand paraphrase of a primary source as surfaced by search, not a verified read of the original table/filing. Confidence ratings reflect both source authority and how directly the number could be checked.

## 2. Ranking (b): Software-addressable vehicle count (penetration- and lock-in-adjusted)

This re-orders the same verticals by how much *genuinely open, incrementally winnable* vehicle count each represents to an FMS vendor today — i.e., raw count discounted by (i) current telematics/FMS penetration and (ii) competitive/compliance lock-in that makes switching costly regardless of penetration.

| Rank | Segment | Raw-count rank | Why it moves | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Government & municipal** | 3 | Largest vehicle base combined with the **lowest documented penetration** (17.5%→29.8%, 2018-2023, still well under the ~53% all-segment average) and comparatively fragmented incumbent share (no single vendor dominates via mandate the way ELD vendors dominate trucking) — genuinely the largest pool of *unconnected* government vehicles. | Berg Insight government-penetration figures (above) |
| 2 | **Construction (vehicles)** | 4 | Samsara's own investor-day disclosure that only **13% of construction equipment** is connected (equipment, not vehicles specifically, but directionally consistent with construction's low telematics maturity) plus Motive naming it a fastest-growing vertical (10% of ARR, rising) — large base, low current attach, minimal single-vendor lock-in. | Samsara Investor Day 2026; Motive S-1 |

| 4 | **Private fleets / wholesale & retail distribution** | 2 | Still growing (NPTC: nearly half the truck market, private fleets added ~90,000 power units recently) and less uniformly ELD-mandate-saturated than for-hire trucking at the smaller end, but large fleets in this segment are already well-instrumented — net addressable slice is meaningfully smaller than the raw 3.4M figure implies. | FleetOwner/NPTC 2025 Benchmarking Survey |

| 6 | **For-hire trucking / freight transportation** | 1 | **Largest raw count (~3.9M) but the most saturated and lock-in-heavy segment** — the ELD mandate already forced near-universal adoption of *some* telematics box, so the competitive game is vendor-switching (Motive vs. Samsara vs. Geotab vs. Omnitracs/Solera) inside an existing installed base, not converting unconnected vehicles. | 7 | **Last-mile delivery & gig/courier fleets** | 9 | Large raw count but structurally **closed** — Amazon, FedEx, and UPS run carrier-owned or carrier-mandated in-house/vendor telematics stacks; a third-party FMS vendor cannot sell into most of this base the way it can sell into an independent trucking or construction fleet. | Amazon DSP program structure; general market knowledge of parcel-carrier telematics ownership |

**Key finding requested by the brief:** the two rankings diverge most sharply on **for-hire trucking**, which is #1 by raw count but drops to #6 by software-addressable count — because the ELD mandate already saturated it with *some* telematics, turning the opportunity into a vendor-switching fight rather than new-logo penetration. The mirror-image case is **government and construction**, which both move *up* between the two rankings because they combine large vehicle populations with genuinely low current penetration (documented directly for government via Berg Insight; more qualitatively for construction via Samsara's equipment-connection disclosure).

## 3. TOP 5 recommendation (raw vehicle count basis)

### 1. For-hire trucking / freight transportation (~3.9M power units, FMCSA MCMIS via FleetOwner 2025)
**Operating model:** Vehicles run scheduled or on-demand line-haul, LTL, or final-mile routes, often crossing state lines and subject to FMCSA hours-of-service rules; a single tractor-trailer can log 400-600+ miles a day. **Economic buyer:** at small carriers, the owner-operator or dispatcher; at mid-size and large carriers, a dedicated VP/Director of Safety or VP of Maintenance controls the telematics/ELD budget, with the CFO gating multi-year contracts. **Dominant pain:** compliance (hours-of-service, ELD data integrity), driver safety and insurance/claims cost, and fuel + maintenance cost control — 2025 fleet-manager surveys rank fuel costs, safety/crash reduction (40% citing it as a rising priority), and maintenance spend (33%) as the top recurring cost pressures. Source: Automotive Fleet fleet-manager cost-priorities coverage (via search snippet, exact article not directly opened this session — treat pain-point framing as **medium confidence**); ELD mandate context per FMCSA.

### 2. Private fleets / wholesale & retail distribution, incl. food & beverage (~3.4M power units, FMCSA MCMIS via FleetOwner 2025)
**Operating model:** Company-owned trucks and vans deliver the parent company's own product to stores, restaurants, or end customers on repeating regional routes (e.g., a bakery's or beverage distributor's own delivery fleet) rather than hauling freight for hire. **Economic buyer:** typically a Director/VP of Transportation or Logistics reporting into supply chain or operations, not a standalone fleet department — fleet spend is viewed as a cost-of-goods-delivered line, so ROI must tie to delivery cost-per-stop or on-time performance. **Dominant pain:** the same fuel/maintenance/safety pressures as for-hire trucking, compounded by delivery-window/customer-service metrics (on-time-in-full) and — per NPTC's 2025 survey — capacity growth (private fleets are adding power units and taking share from for-hire carriers, meaning many of these teams are scaling fast and under-resourced to manage the growth).

### 3. Government & municipal (federal + state + local, excl. pupil transportation/transit)
**Operating model:** A heterogeneous mix — public-works trucks (plowing, paving, utility maintenance), inspector/code-enforcement sedans, police cruisers, and general-purpose pool vehicles — usually running short in-town routes rather than long-haul miles, but held in service far longer than commercial fleets. **Economic buyer:** a civil-service Fleet Manager or Director of Public Works/Fleet Services, whose budget is set annually by council/legislature and who typically must buy through cooperative-purchasing vehicles (Sourcewell, NASPO ValuePoint, HGACBuy) rather than a direct RFP — this is a structurally different, slower buying motion than private-sector procurement. **Dominant pain:** aging fleet and replacement budgeting — the average government vehicle is reported at **7.4 years old vs. 3-5 years for commercial fleets**, driven by 18-36-month procurement cycles, competitive-bid requirements, and constrained capital budgets. Sources: NASPO, "Dude, Where's My Car? Governments Struggle with Vehicle Procurements": https://www.naspo.org/news/dude-wheres-my-car-governments-struggle-with-vehicle-procurements/ ; Government Fleet, "Aging Fleets, Replacement Budgeting Top Concerns for Fleets": https://www.government-fleet.com/articles/benchmark-statistics-among-government-fleets — **Confidence: Medium** (both are named trade/association sources, paraphrased via search).

### 4. Construction (vehicles — trucks, vans, pickups; heavy equipment tracked separately by the parallel workstream)
**Operating model:** Pickups and vocational trucks (dump, mixer, boom, flatbed) shuttle crews, materials, and tools between a home yard and rotating, often-remote job sites, with heavy day-to-day idling and short-radius, low-mileage-but-high-wear-and-tear duty cycles. **Economic buyer:** typically an Operations Manager, Fleet/Equipment Manager, or — at smaller contractors — the owner directly; increasingly, safety/insurance stakeholders are involved because commercial-auto and general-liability premiums are directly tied to driver behavior data. **Dominant pain:** utilization and job-costing visibility (which truck/crew is where, billing time correctly to which job) and vehicle/equipment security given frequent theft from job sites — Motive names construction one of its fastest-growing verticals (10% of ARR, rising), and Samsara's own data shows construction as one of the least-instrumented verticals today (only 13% of construction *equipment* connected, per its 2026 investor day — a proxy, since that stat covers equipment rather than vehicles specifically). Confidence: **Medium** on the vertical's growth/priority signal (primary-source-derived), **Low** on the specific "job-costing/theft" pain framing (reasoned from general industry commentary, not a single cited survey).

### 5. Field services / trades (HVAC, plumbing, electrical, pest, landscaping, facilities/janitorial)
**Operating model:** Vans and pickups run a dispatcher-assigned daily route of multiple short service calls (installs, repairs, inspections) at customer sites, with the vehicle effectively serving as a mobile parts warehouse and workstation for a single technician. **Economic buyer:** for the 1-10-vehicle majority, the owner/GM directly; above roughly 10-15 vehicles, a dedicated Operations Manager or Fleet Coordinator emerges, usually reporting to the GM/owner rather than a standalone fleet department — this is a fundamentally SMB-buyer motion, distinct from the enterprise sales cycles that work for trucking, government, or utilities. **Dominant pain:** dispatch chaos and reactive scheduling — dispatchers "juggling spreadsheets, last-minute calls, appointment management issues, double bookings, and drive-time detours," compounded by the cost of unnecessary miles and the customer-experience damage when a vehicle breaks down mid-route and a job has to be reassigned on the fly. Source: field-service-management industry commentary (Geotab, BuildOps, and peers, via search snippet) — **Confidence: Medium** (consistent theme across multiple independent vendor/industry sources, but no single named survey with a year was pinned down).

**Just missed the cut:** Utilities & telecom (concentrated, well-documented mega-fleets like AT&T's 86,099 vehicles, but the *sector-wide* raw count is smaller than government/construction/field-services once the handful of headline fleets are excluded) and last-mile/gig delivery (large raw count, but structurally closed to third-party FMS vendors — see Ranking (b)).

## 4. Taxonomy & double-counting notes

- **Private fleets vs. wholesale/retail distribution vs. food & beverage**: same physical trucks viewed through three lenses (ownership structure, NAICS industry, product category). Collapsed into one row (#2) rather than triple-counted.
- **Construction vs. field services vs. utilities/telecom**: all three buy similar vocational trucks (bucket trucks, dump trucks, cable vans); kept as separate rows per the requested taxonomy, but Automotive Fleet's own 2026 Top 300 commentary explicitly notes "telecom infrastructure contractors... construction-support organizations" appearing together — exactly this ambiguity playing out in a primary source.
- **For-hire trucking vs. last-mile/gig delivery**: UPS, FedEx, and Amazon Logistics vehicles could independently qualify as either. Row #1 = line-haul/LTL/truckload backbone; row #9 (last-mile) = final-mile/parcel/DSP layer — but summing both rows would double-count these companies' fleets.
- **Government & municipal vs. pupil transportation & transit vs. emergency services/EMS**: legally "government" in most cases (school buses are usually district-owned; ambulances often run by municipal fire departments), but kept separate because they are functionally distinct FMS buying centers. A true "total government vehicle count" would need to sum rows #3, #7 (school-bus portion), and part of #10 (EMS) without double-counting NAFA's 4.8M figure, which likely already includes some of these.
- **Non-powered assets excluded entirely**: per the revised scope, trailers, containers, heavy equipment, forklifts, generators, light towers, and ag equipment are owned by a parallel researcher and are not counted, ranked, or estimated anywhere in this document, even where a segment (construction, agriculture, towing/rental) would look much larger if they were included.
